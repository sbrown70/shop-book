Four rules that protect the cam timing and the valves

  1. The crank and cam only turn together. Any time the chain is off, don't turn either one more than a few degrees. With the cam stopped, a piston coming up can meet a valve that is held open.
  2. Turn the engine clockwise, as seen from the front. That's the way it runs. It keeps the chain slack on the same side so the timing dots read true, and it tightens the bolt you're turning with instead of unscrewing it. A little backward by accident won't hurt anything. If you overshoot a mark, back up well past it and come forward again.
  3. Everything goes back where it came from, facing the same way: rod caps, main caps, bearing shells, bolts, the slinger and the fuel pump eccentric.
  4. Assembly lube on bearing faces only. The backs of the shells, the saddles and the caps' bores stay clean and dry.
Why the cam timing comes back exactly

Cam timing is set by the sprocket keyways and the timing dots, not by where the cam happens to sit. If the chain goes back on with the dots lined up the way you photograph them in Phase 2, the timing is the same as before. You only need to re-degree if you change the cam, the sprockets or an adjustable setting.

Parts and tools

Parts

  • Two-piece rubber rear main seal for a 289/302
  • Front cover gasket set with a new front crank seal
  • Oil pan gasket set
  • Oil pump and pickup gaskets
  • Water pump and fuel pump gaskets
  • Engine assembly lube
  • Oil-resistant RTV and anaerobic gasket maker
  • Brake cleaner or other solvent
  • Oil and filter
  • Main and rod bearings, only if inspection says so

Tools

  • Torque wrench that reads 10–90 ft-lb
  • Feeler gauge set, or a dial indicator with magnetic base
  • Damper puller (bolts into the hub) and damper installer
  • Plastigage, green (0.001–0.003 in.)
  • 2–3 in. outside micrometer reading to 0.0001 in. (optional, for checking journal taper and out-of-round)
  • Eight pairs of 5/16 in. hose pieces, about 3 in. long
  • Paint pen and number punches
  • Plastic scraper, brass pick, brass punch
  • Soft-face mallet, pry bars
  • Flywheel lock or a stout bar for the ring gear
  • Oil pump priming tool (optional)

Why the rods come off first

You have to turn the crank to reach every rod nut. With the chain still on, the cam turns with it, so the valves and pistons stay in step and can't meet.

Phase 4

Timing set off

While it's off

Check the chain. If it's visibly sloppy or the cam sprocket teeth are worn into hooks, a new set installed dot to dot is reasonable. A worn chain was retarding the cam slightly, so a new one restores the timing the engine was built with.

Phase 5

Crank out

From here until Phase 8

Don't turn the cam, and don't turn the crank more than a few degrees.

Torques are for stock fasteners with clean, oiled threads. Aftermarket bolts such as ARP use their maker's spec. Published figures for the stock rod nuts vary between 19–24 and 22–25 ft-lb, which is why the steps aim for 22–24. The bearing clearance figures are a rule of thumb for a used stock engine. When a factory shop manual for your car is at hand, its numbers win.
